For two decades, the Honda Ridgeline has been the favorite punching bag of "real" truck guys who think a unibody frame is basically a floral pattern. Turns out, the critics were right all along—but the Ridgeline is laughing all the way to the suburban grocery store.

Retired designer Don Herner recently confirmed that Honda’s pickup didn’t start as a corporate mandate to crush Ford or Chevy. It began as a rogue side project sketched in the shadows, officially dubbed the "Advanced Capabilities Vehicle."

Honda’s priority wasn’t out-towing a freight train; it was beating the contemporary Toyota Tacoma in legroom and interior comfort. It was essentially a crossover designed from the inside out, a strategy usually reserved for family haulers rather than gritty workhorses.

- The Ridgeline shares its DNA with the Odyssey minivan, which explains why it handles like a dream and tows like a polite suggestion.
- Designers originally mocked up 35-inch tires to sell the rugged fantasy, but engineers killed the vibe because Honda lacked a transmission robust enough to turn them.
- To keep the 3.5-liter V6 from screaming for mercy, they opted for smaller wheels to preserve torque at the ground.

The 1,100-pound bed limit might have sparked a million memes, but for Honda, the compromise was worth the "SUV interior size." It wasn’t an identity crisis; it was a deliberate middle finger to traditional truck architecture in favor of daily livability.

If you’re still making "fake truck" jokes, you’re just stating the obvious. Honda never wanted to join the club; they just wanted to build a better Odyssey with a porch.
