For decades, the art world’s gatekeepers operated under a boring, self-imposed rule: Black art had to be figurative to be "relevant." If it didn't explicitly depict the struggle through recognizable faces, critics often acted like it wasn't "political" enough to matter.

MoMA PS1’s new survey, "Hard Art," effectively tosses that stale playbook into a commercial-grade shredder. Featuring over 40 artists spanning from 1969 to today, the exhibition proves that Black abstraction isn't just a niche—it’s a high-voltage marathon of innovation that has been running for over half a century.

The show’s title pays homage to Peter Bradley, the painter who curated the legendary 1971 "De Luxe Show" in a converted Houston movie theater. For Bradley, "hard" wasn’t just a stylistic descriptor; it represented the literal grit required to produce high-concept art while living on the margins of a white-dominated industry.

- **The Myth:** The narrow assumption that Black artists were "required" to stick to realism to have a legitimate voice.
- **The Reality:** A vibrant legacy of Color Field pioneers and experimentalists who refused to be boxed in by expectations.
- **The Venue:** MoMA PS1, which is revisiting its own 1980 roots in the movement.

This isn't some dusty, academic history lesson. It’s a sharp reminder that identity isn't a stylistic prison, and a splash of color can be just as radical as a protest poster. 

Go for the textures, but stay for the realization that we’ve been sleeping on these masters for far too long.
