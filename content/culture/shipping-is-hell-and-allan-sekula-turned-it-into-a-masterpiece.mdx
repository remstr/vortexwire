The next time you’re tracking a $15 phone case across the Pacific, remember that global commerce isn’t a magic trick. It’s a massive, gritty, and often soul-crushing operation involving people who haven’t seen dry land or a decent steak in weeks.

Photographer Allan Sekula spent seven years documenting this industrial underworld. His magnum opus, "Fish Story," is finally making its New York debut, and it’s a far cry from your typical high-society gallery fare.

Forget the high-gloss aesthetic of a luxury travel magazine. Sekula’s work is the ultimate anti-travelogue, trading sun-drenched cruise decks for the rust-stained reality of dockworkers, sailors, and the heavy machinery of the deep.

It’s a sprawling narrative that treats steel shipping containers like the literal building blocks of modern civilization. Critics are calling it the greatest seafaring epic in contemporary art, and for once, the hyperbole is actually earned.

Why this industrial deep-dive matters:
- It exposes the "invisible" labor force that fuels our global supply chains while living on the margins.
- The project spans the globe, from the massive shipyards of Korea to the sprawling, automated ports of Rotterdam.
- It rejects the idea that technology has made the ocean irrelevant, proving the sea remains the world's primary highway.

Sekula managed to turn cold logistics into high art without ever losing the human element. If you want to understand how the world actually functions—and why it occasionally grinds to a halt—this is the essential view from the engine room.
