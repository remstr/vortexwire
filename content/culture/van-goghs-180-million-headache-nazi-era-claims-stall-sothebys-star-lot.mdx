Sotheby’s expected a record-breaking payday for Vincent van Gogh’s "Blossoming Chestnut Trees," but a looming legal battle is making a severed ear look like a minor inconvenience. The heirs of the painting’s former Jewish owner are demanding the auction house pull the work before it hits the block in Manhattan.

They argue the 1890 masterpiece wasn't a voluntary sale, but a transaction made under the crushing weight of Nazi persecution in 1936. At an estimated $180 million, it’s the most expensive Van Gogh ever to hit an auction floor—if it actually makes it to the gavel.

- **The Conflict:** Heirs say the family’s publishing empire was decimated by the Third Reich, forcing a "duress" sale in Amsterdam to survive.
- **The Ultimatum:** Their lawyer is challenging Sotheby’s to prove the sale was fair market value and entirely voluntary, even without Nazi interference.
- **The Stakes:** The painting is the centerpiece of the marquee fall auctions and is currently backed by a massive financial guarantee from Sotheby’s.

The work spent decades hidden in the private Blaquier-Arrieta collection, often described by art insiders as "the stuff of legend." That legend is getting complicated as lawyers invoke the 1998 Washington Principles regarding Nazi-looted assets.

Sotheby’s says they are committed to a "mutually satisfactory resolution." In high-stakes auction-speak, that usually means a frantic behind-the-scenes scramble to keep a nine-figure commission from evaporating into thin air.
